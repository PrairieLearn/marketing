This question came up recently: when students take an exam in PrairieLearn,
should they find out right away whether each answer is correct?

My usual answer is yes, for most auto-graded questions. Let students submit an
answer, see whether it is correct, and try again for reduced credit.

But I would not frame this as "immediate feedback is always better." The details
matter. What kind of feedback do students see? Can they act on it? How much
credit can they recover? Is the question a conceptual multiple-choice question,
a numerical problem, or a programming task? And how are we handling exam
security?

The interesting part is not simply telling a student "correct" or "incorrect."
It is giving them a chance to notice a mistake, think again, and show more of
what they know.

## Why give feedback during an exam?

Paper exams made delayed feedback feel normal because there was no practical
alternative. Students handed in their work, staff graded it, and feedback arrived
days later.

Computer-based exams give us more choices.

For an auto-graded question, a student might submit an answer, learn that it is
incorrect, reconsider their work, and submit a better answer for partial credit.
That is sometimes called an _answer-until-correct_ format. It is more than a
running score. It gives students another opportunity to demonstrate
understanding.

This can matter especially for complex work. A student may understand the
physics or the algorithm but make an arithmetic error, use the wrong sign, or
write code with one small syntax mistake. On a traditional single-submission
exam, that small error can hide a lot of what the student actually knows.

I do not think every mistake should be fully recoverable. But I do think exams
should be designed so that one small mistake does not erase evidence of
substantial learning.

## What does the research say?

A fair amount of research on answer-until-correct exams comes from
multiple-choice testing, including exams that use scratch-off answer cards.
Students choose an answer, learn immediately whether it is right, and can try
again for less credit.

That research gives us a few useful takeaways.

First, allowing another attempt predictably raises scores. That does not mean
students learned more during the exam. It means the grading policy recognizes
partial knowledge and the ability to recover from an initial mistake.

Second, the additional attempts are not just random guessing. Studies have found
that students who perform well overall are also more likely to recover after an
incorrect first answer. The extra information can make an exam more reliable,
not less.

Third, students generally like the format. They appreciate knowing where they
stand and having a chance to earn back some credit. At the same time, some
students find repeated incorrect feedback stressful. Both things can be true.

The research on whether _immediate_ feedback produces better long-term learning
than delayed feedback is more mixed. In some settings, delayed feedback helps
retention just as much or more. So I would not argue that immediate correctness
feedback is a magic learning intervention.

Its value during an exam is more practical: students can use it while the
assessment is still happening.

## A note about anxiety

The concern I hear most often is that immediate feedback will make students
anxious.

It certainly can. A string of wrong answers can be discouraging, especially on a
high-stakes exam. But the available research does not suggest that immediate
feedback generally harms students with higher test anxiety. Many students prefer
it because they can correct mistakes instead of spending the rest of the exam
wondering whether they got something wrong.

The design of the exam matters much more than whether a green or red indicator
appears on the screen.

A few things help:

- Spread points across several questions instead of putting too much weight on
  one brittle question.
- Include some accessible opportunities to earn points.
- Use partial-credit curves that allow students to recover from early mistakes.
- Tell students they can save an answer without grading it yet. They do not have
  to get feedback immediately after every question.
- Offer practice exams so students can get comfortable with the format before the
  stakes are high.
- Use lower-stakes exams and second-chance opportunities when possible.

A student who knows they have another chance to demonstrate learning is usually
in a much better position than a student who feels that one bad sitting
determines everything.

## Programming questions are different

For programming questions, I am even more in favor of allowing students to get
feedback and retry.

Programming involves debugging. A student can have the right overall approach
and still have a missing parenthesis, an off-by-one error, or a small mismatch
with the required interface. If we care about whether students can write and
debug working code, then some opportunity to compile, test, and revise is part
of the skill we are assessing.

Of course, an autograder can become too helpful. Unlimited graded submissions
with detailed feedback on every hidden test can turn the assessment into a
guessing game.

A reasonable middle ground is:

- Let students run their own code freely.
- Give them visible examples or public tests.
- Use hidden tests to check broader correctness.
- Allow a small number of graded attempts for decreasing credit.
- Give useful but incomplete feedback.
- Add a short delay between graded submissions if rapid guessing becomes a
  problem.

The goal is to support genuine debugging without turning the grader into an
oracle.

## What about exam security?

Immediate feedback does reveal information. If students can keep trying a
multiple-choice question until they get it right, they eventually know the
answer. Detailed feedback can reveal even more.

But this is mostly an item-exposure problem, not a reason to eliminate feedback
entirely.

For asynchronous exams, the important protections are good question variants,
question pools, careful control of when solutions are released, and a testing
environment that supports secure delivery. Randomization is not magic, and
variants need to be comparable in difficulty. Still, if different students
receive meaningfully different versions of a question, a shared answer is much
less useful.

For most PrairieLearn exams, I would keep the in-exam feedback focused on what a
student can productively use right then. Save full solutions and richer
explanations for after the exam window closes.

## My default recommendation

For most auto-graded PrairieLearn exams, I would:

1. Enable real-time grading.
2. Allow a small number of attempts with decreasing credit.
3. Be more generous on complex numerical and programming questions than on
   simple recognition questions.
4. Let students choose whether to grade an answer now or save it for later.
5. Give feedback that is useful but does not reveal the full solution.
6. Use generated variants and question pools to support exam security.
7. Release fuller explanations after the exam window closes.

There are exceptions. Sometimes feedback on one question would reveal an answer
needed for a later question. Sometimes an exam has unusually strong security
requirements and not enough variation between students. PrairieLearn supports
turning real-time grading on or off for particular parts of an assessment.

Still, I would treat disabling feedback as the exception, not the default.

Computer-based exams let us move beyond the old paper-exam model of "write
everything down, hand it in, and wait." A well-designed assessment can give
students feedback, let them repair small mistakes, and still measure the
learning we care about.

That is a better use of the technology, and usually a fairer exam.
