How do we know what students can do independently when AI can complete much of the work we assign?

For many instructors, this question has become a reason to rethink assessment. We want students to demonstrate their understanding under conditions where access to AI and other outside resources can be controlled. But arranging those conditions takes time: finding a room, recruiting proctors, handling accommodations, and scheduling conflict and makeup exams.

A testing center provides shared infrastructure for that work. It gives students a supervised place to demonstrate what they know and gives instructors a service they can rely on across their courses.

This approach has received renewed attention in 2026. In [“Blue Books Are Not the Answer to AI,”](https://www.insidehighered.com/opinion/views/2026/03/19/blue-books-are-not-answer-ai-opinion) Armando Fox and Craig Zilles describe how controlled computer environments and human proctors can protect exam integrity while preserving the benefits of digital assessment. In an [October Teaching newsletter](https://www.chronicle.com/newsletter/teaching/2026-10-08), Beth McMurtrie reports on University of Chicago faculty recommendations for spaces where students can work without AI, including computerized, asynchronous testing facilities. A central theme is the need for institutions to help create the conditions for independent work.

I would love to see more institutions invest in this infrastructure. But an instructor planning next semester’s course needs options that are available now. What can you do if your institution does not have a testing center yet?

## Why a testing center is worth the investment

A well-run testing center brings together several things that instructors would otherwise have to arrange themselves:

- **A controlled testing environment.** For computer-based exams, institution-managed computers and a properly configured firewall can block AI websites and other prohibited sites. Exam access rules restrict students to their assessment and approved materials.
- **Consistent proctoring.** Trained staff check identities, manage personal belongings and prohibited devices, and monitor the room. Security cameras can supplement in-person supervision.
- **Support for accommodations.** The center can coordinate extended time, accessible workstations, and reduced-distraction spaces with the campus accessibility office.
- **Flexible scheduling.** When an exam is designed for delivery across a window, students can choose a time that fits their schedules, reducing conflicts. Approved makeup exams can use the same infrastructure.
- **Less administrative work for course staff.** Instructors do not have to book evening classrooms, recruit proctors, or arrange separate rooms for every accommodated or conflict exam.

A testing center can support paper-based exams too, and PrairieTest can help coordinate their scheduling and administration.

These shared services also make frequent exams and retakes more practical. Our post on [Craig Zilles’s discussion of retakes at scale](https://www.prairielearn.com/about/blog/craig-zilles-retakes-at-scale) explores how testing infrastructure makes those teaching choices easier to sustain.

Computer-based testing adds possibilities I find particularly valuable: generated question variants, interactive questions, programming tasks, and immediate feedback. The [Illinois CBTF best practices](https://cbtf.illinois.edu/about/best-practices) provide guidance on preparing students and assessments for that environment.

## Start with an existing computer lab

Your institution may already have much of the necessary equipment. A departmental computer lab could be a starting point, even if it is available for testing only during certain hours.

I would start with campus IT and the people responsible for the lab. Can its computers be configured for secure exams? Who can provide technical support? Could several courses share trained proctors and a reservation process?

PrairieTest identifies two configurations that provide high security: institution-owned computers with in-person proctoring and either an institutional firewall or LockDown Browser. A dedicated computer-based testing facility (CBTF) is not required to use those configurations. [PrairieTest’s testing-center security guide](https://us.prairietest.com/pt/docs/center/security-center) explains how network restrictions and assessment access controls work together.

A smaller lab may be able to serve a larger course through multiple sessions, provided the exam is designed for that delivery.

Berkeley offers a useful example of starting small. In [“Bootstrapping a Computer-Based Testing Facility Using Shared Lab Space,”](https://sigcsevirtual2026.acm.org/details/sigcse-virtual-2026-papers/40/Bootstrapping-a-Computer-Based-Testing-Facility-Using-Shared-Lab-Space) Armando Fox describes the lessons from a pilot that grew into a service supporting departments across campus. His account emphasizes coordination across campus units and choosing procedures that can grow with the service.

## If a lab is not available, use a proctored classroom

Bring-your-own-device (BYOD) exams let students take a computer-based assessment on their own laptops in a classroom.

For this setup, I would use **LockDown Browser together with active, in-person proctoring**. PrairieTest supports LockDown Browser for exams delivered through PrairieLearn.

Student-owned computers remain outside institutional control. Staff cannot verify everything installed on them or rule out modifications that bypass restrictions. This provides less security than a properly controlled campus computer.

Proctoring needs to reflect that limitation. Plan for enough proctors to circulate throughout the room, see screens, and respond to problems. One person sitting at the front of a large lecture hall cannot provide that coverage.

Before the first exam, I would:

- Run a practice assessment with the same browser and setup.
- Check the room layout, Wi-Fi, and access to power.
- Arrange an alternative for students whose devices cannot run the exam.
- Plan accommodations and train proctors on technical issues and exam procedures.

A [2026 experience report from UC San Diego](https://sigcse2026.sigcse.org/details/sigcse-ts-2026-Papers/72/Designing-and-Implementing-Skill-Tests-at-Scale-Frequent-Computer-Based-Proctored-) describes a practical model: short, frequent, proctored skill tests run by course staff using student laptops and existing campus rooms. It provides an example of organizing assessments without a dedicated testing facility.

Our [SIGCSE 2024 BYOD study](https://doi.org/10.1145/3626252.3630809) examined proctoring without lockdown browsers. We found a score advantage on non-coding questions in unproctored exams compared with Zoom-proctored exams, but no statistically significant score difference between Zoom and in-person proctoring. The study did not evaluate today’s LockDown Browser configurations or establish that those proctoring environments are equally secure.

## Can online proctoring fill the gap?

Online proctoring has substantial limitations for exams requiring high security. A webcam shows only part of the student’s surroundings. Another device, notes, or another person may be outside its view. A lockdown browser alone cannot address those possibilities.

I would prioritize an in-person option when the goal is to verify independent work without access to AI or other prohibited resources. The [PrairieTest exam security guide](https://us.prairietest.com/pt/docs/course/exam-security-levels) compares the available configurations and their limitations.

## What about students taking exams at different times?

Asynchronous testing can still take place in a supervised environment. However, flexible scheduling requires attention to what students might share after taking an exam.

Meaningful question randomization, question pools, practice opportunities, and decisions about when to release solutions all matter. A secure room alone does not solve question exposure.

That deserves a separate post: **“How can exams be secure when students take them at different times?”** Our [post on immediate feedback](https://www.prairielearn.com/about/blog/immediate-feedback-on-exams) touches on some of these choices.

## Building toward a testing center

Start with a manageable pilot and identify what your courses could share: a room, a proctoring team, scheduling, or a process for accommodations. Document the staffing, space, and coordination it requires.

That experience can improve the next exam and help make the case for a campus testing service. The need is already shared across courses; organizing the support together is a practical next step.

For institutions considering that investment, these papers provide useful starting points:

- [Every University Should Have a Computer-Based Testing Facility](https://zilles.cs.illinois.edu/papers/zilles_csedu_cbtf_2019.pdf), Zilles, West, Herman, and Bretl, CSEDU 2019.
- [Reflections on 10 Years of Operating a Computer-Based Testing Facility: Lessons Learned, Best Practices](https://peer.asee.org/reflections-on-10-years-of-operating-a-computer-based-testing-facility-lessons-learned-best-practices), Sosnowski and colleagues, ASEE 2024.
