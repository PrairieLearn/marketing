How do we know what students can do independently when [AI can complete much of the work we assign](https://peer.asee.org/61062)?

For many instructors, this question has become a reason to rethink assessment, and finding a workable approach takes time and creativity. Some are bringing back oral exams or using projects to assess students’ understanding. These approaches can be valuable, but they can be difficult to scale and may not suit every course. For instructors using supervised exams, there is also the work of finding a room, recruiting proctors, handling accommodations, and scheduling conflict and makeup exams.

A testing center provides shared infrastructure for that work. This approach has received renewed attention in 2026. In [“Blue Books Are Not the Answer to AI,”](https://www.insidehighered.com/opinion/views/2026/03/19/blue-books-are-not-answer-ai-opinion) Armando Fox and Craig Zilles describe how controlled computer environments and human proctors can protect exam integrity while preserving the benefits of digital assessment. In an [October Teaching newsletter](https://www.chronicle.com/newsletter/teaching/2026-10-08), Beth McMurtrie reports on University of Chicago faculty recommendations for spaces where students can work without AI, including computerized, asynchronous testing facilities. A central theme is the need for institutions to help create the conditions for independent work.

## First, why use a testing center?

A well-run testing center brings together several things that instructors would otherwise have to arrange themselves:

- **A controlled testing environment.** For computer-based exams, institution-managed computers and a properly configured firewall can block AI websites and other prohibited sites. Exam access rules restrict students to their assessment and approved materials.
- **Consistent proctoring.** Trained staff check identities, manage personal belongings and prohibited devices, and monitor the room. Security cameras can supplement in-person supervision.
- **Support for accommodations.** The center can coordinate extended time, accessible workstations, and reduced-distraction spaces with the campus accessibility office.
- **Flexible scheduling.** When an exam is designed for delivery across a window, students can choose a time that fits their schedules, reducing conflicts. Approved makeup exams can use the same infrastructure.
- **Less administrative work for course staff.** Instructors do not have to book evening classrooms, recruit proctors, or arrange separate rooms for every accommodated or conflict exam.
- **Support for frequent exams and retakes.** Shared testing services make these teaching choices more practical. Our post on [Craig Zilles’s discussion of retakes at scale](https://www.prairielearn.com/about/blog/craig-zilles-retakes-at-scale) explores how testing infrastructure makes them easier to sustain.

A testing center can support both paper-based and computer-based exams, and PrairieTest can help coordinate scheduling and administration for either format. Computer-based testing opens up possibilities I find particularly valuable when designing assessments. A programming exam can ask students to write, run, and debug code. A numerical question can give them feedback and a chance to catch a mistake. The [Illinois CBTF best practices](https://cbtf.illinois.edu/about/best-practices) provide guidance on preparing students and assessments for that environment.

I would love to see more institutions invest in this infrastructure. But an instructor planning next semester’s course needs options that are available now. What can you do if your institution does not have a testing center yet?

## Start with an existing computer lab

Your institution may already have much of the necessary equipment in a departmental computer lab, even if the space is available for testing only during certain hours. Campus IT and the people responsible for the lab can help you explore what is feasible.

With IT’s support, those computers can be configured to restrict access to prohibited resources during exams. PrairieTest supports either an institutional firewall or LockDown Browser on institution-owned computers, together with in-person proctoring. For a shared lab, [installing LockDown Browser](https://us.prairietest.com/pt/docs/center/respondus-lockdown-browser) and using it during PrairieLearn exam sessions may be the simpler way to start.

As more courses begin using the lab, that experience can help make the case for a dedicated computer-based testing facility (CBTF). At that stage, it may also be easier to justify investing in a firewall configured for exams; [PrairieTest’s testing-center security guide](https://us.prairietest.com/pt/docs/center/security-center) explains how that setup works.

Berkeley offers an encouraging example of that progression. Its pilot began in shared lab space in 2023; three years later, its CBTF operation was twenty times larger and serving departments across campus. [Armando Fox’s account of that experience](https://sigcsevirtual2026.acm.org/details/sigcse-virtual-2026-papers/40/Bootstrapping-a-Computer-Based-Testing-Facility-Using-Shared-Lab-Space) highlights two lessons for getting started: coordinate across campus units, and choose procedures that can grow with the service.

## If a lab is not available, use a proctored classroom

[Bring-your-own-device (BYOD) exams](https://doi.org/10.1145/3626252.3630809) let students take a computer-based assessment on their own laptops in a classroom. For PrairieLearn exams delivered through PrairieTest, I would use LockDown Browser together with active, in-person proctoring.

This setup provides less security than a properly controlled campus computer because staff cannot verify everything installed on student-owned devices or rule out modifications that bypass restrictions. To account for that limitation, plan for enough proctors to circulate throughout the room, see screens, and respond to problems. One person sitting at the front of a large lecture hall cannot provide that coverage.

Before the first exam, I would:

- Run a practice assessment with the same browser and setup.
- Check the room layout, Wi-Fi, and access to power.
- Arrange an alternative for students whose devices cannot run the exam.
- Plan accommodations and train proctors on technical issues and exam procedures.

At UC San Diego, course staff used student laptops and existing campus rooms to offer weekly, 10-minute proctored skill tests in a large introductory programming course. Their [2026 experience report](https://sigcse2026.sigcse.org/details/sigcse-ts-2026-Papers/72/Designing-and-Implementing-Skill-Tests-at-Scale-Frequent-Computer-Based-Proctored-) provides a model for making frequent assessments practical without a dedicated testing facility.

## Can online proctoring fill the gap?

Online proctoring has substantial limitations for exams requiring high security. A webcam shows only part of the student’s surroundings. Another device, notes, or another person may be outside its view. A lockdown browser alone cannot address those possibilities.

I would prioritize an [in-person option](https://us.prairietest.com/pt/docs/course/exam-security-levels) when the goal is to verify independent work without access to AI or other prohibited resources. A shared room and proctoring team can make this practical even before a dedicated testing center is available.

## What about students taking exams at different times?

We have studied asynchronous exams in supervised testing centers, looking at [performance trends across an exam window](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3051457.3051465), [how students choose exam times](https://doi.org/10.1002/jee.20292), and [score differences between synchronous and asynchronous exams](https://doi.org/10.1145/3328778.3366859). Together, these studies have informed recommendations for reducing the advantage students might gain from conversations about exam content, including [question randomization and question pools](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3231644.3231664). With a well-designed exam and a controlled testing environment, I would expect any remaining advantage from those conversations to be much smaller than what students could gain from using AI or other prohibited resources during a synchronous exam in a less secure setting.

## Build on what other campuses have learned

If you are trying to make this happen on your campus, find other instructors who need the same support. A proposal for a shared pilot, with specific courses, a room, and a plan for staffing, gives your department or teaching center something concrete to work with.

The experiences of other campuses can help you prepare that proposal and plan what comes next:

- **Getting a center running:** [One Solution to Addressing Assessment Logistical Problems: An Experience Setting Up and Operating an In-person Testing Center](https://doi.org/10.1145/3626252.3630902), Downey, Miller, Silva, and Zilles (SIGCSE 2024), shares UC Riverside’s setup decisions and lessons from its first two quarters.
- **Running and expanding a service:** [Reflections on 10 Years of Operating a Computer-Based Testing Facility: Lessons Learned, Best Practices](https://peer.asee.org/reflections-on-10-years-of-operating-a-computer-based-testing-facility-lessons-learned-best-practices), Sosnowski and colleagues (ASEE 2024), describes how Illinois adapted its staffing, scheduling, and policies as demand grew.
- **Making the institutional case:** [Every University Should Have a Computer-Based Testing Facility](https://zilles.cs.illinois.edu/papers/zilles_csedu_cbtf_2019.pdf), Zilles, West, Herman, and Bretl (CSEDU 2019), connects the investment in testing infrastructure to the assessment practices it makes possible.

For additional examples, Michigan’s [account of its shared-space CBTF pilot](https://caennews.engin.umich.edu/computer-based-testing-facility-cbtf-pilot-continues/) and UBC’s [faculty guidance](https://cbtf.ubc.ca/faculty) offer practical perspectives on introducing and supporting the service.

There is also a community of instructors and staff who run testing centers, with meetings every other week and a Slack channel for exchanging ideas and practical advice. Their experience with staffing, space, and campus coordination can help you turn an idea for next semester into support that several courses can rely on.
